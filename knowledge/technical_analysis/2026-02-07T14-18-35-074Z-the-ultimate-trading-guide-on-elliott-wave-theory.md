## Key points
- Elliott Wave Theory uses fractal wave patterns to predict market trends based on collective investor psychology, with self-similar structures appearing across different timeframes
- Markets move in a 5-3 wave structure: five waves (1-2-3-4-5) with the trend (impulsive move) followed by three waves (A-B-C) against it (corrective move)
- Impulsive waves contain three impulsive sub-waves (1, 3, 5) and two corrective sub-waves (2, 4), with Wave 3 typically the strongest and most extended wave where bulk of profits are made
- A trend reversal is signaled when a corrective wave breaks below the low point of the previous Wave 4 (uptrend) or Wave 6 (after full 5-3 cycle)
- Wave 2 and Wave 4 often retrace 50-61.8% of their preceding impulsive wave using Fibonacci ratios, while Wave 3 and Wave 5 often extend to 161.8% of Wave 1
- Three common corrective patterns: zigzag (sharp, steep decline), flat (sideways consolidation), and triangle (narrowing price movement)
- Elliott Wave is best used with confluence tools like Fibonacci retracements, moving averages, RSI, and multi-timeframe analysis rather than in isolation
- Only count waves when clean wave structures are visible; if patterns are unclear, use different technical tools to avoid misinterpretation
- Wave extensions (especially in Wave 3) create longer structures beyond usual range, while wave truncations occur when Wave 5 fails to surpass Wave 3, indicating weak momentum
- Elliott Wave Theory is more art than science and requires flexibility—trends with 7 or 9 waves are common and don't always follow exact counts
- Avoid psychological bias by zooming out to higher timeframes and creating contingency plans for both bullish and bearish scenarios
- Spotting Wave 3 early offers prime entry opportunity as it represents the strongest directional move with greatest profit potential
- Complex corrective waves like double or triple zigzags indicate multiple corrective phases requiring deeper understanding of market behavior
